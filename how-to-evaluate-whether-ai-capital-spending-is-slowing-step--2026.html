How to Evaluate Whether AI Capital Spending Is Slowing (Step-by-Step)

Share

Within hours of Microsoft reporting $41 billion of capital spending for its June 2026 quarter, one set of posts called it proof the AI boom was speeding up, and another called it the top. Both were reading the same number, and neither asked the question that settles the argument. To judge whether AI capital spending is slowing, measure how fast spending grows rather than how large it is. Then correct the figure for lease accounting and chip prices, and test it against revenue and free cash flow. Spending can set a new high every quarter and still be slowing.

The first pass through this method takes about 90 minutes per company. Once you have a spreadsheet template, it goes faster. Difficulty is intermediate: you need to find capex on a cash-flow statement, but you don't need to build models.

The short version

AI capex is slowing when its growth rate falls, even while dollar totals keep rising. Classify each company as accelerating, decelerating, or contracting, using both quarter-over-quarter and year-over-year comparisons, after adjusting for leases and component inflation. Based on mid-2026 disclosures from Microsoft, Meta, Alphabet, and Amazon, spending is still expanding in absolute terms. Deceleration, not contraction, is the more credible near-term scenario.

What you need before you start

  • Access to SEC EDGAR, which is free and holds every 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K, including cash-flow statements and property-and-equipment notes
  • The latest earnings release and call transcript for each company you track
  • A spreadsheet with one row per quarter
  • Optional: a data terminal. Koyfin charges $0 for its Free tier, $39 a month for Plus, $79 for Premium, $209 for Advisor Core, and $299 for Advisor Pro.

The free tier plus EDGAR covers everything below. US earnings calls usually land in the small hours of the morning IST, so the transcript is often the more practical source for Indian readers.

Step 1: Define "slowing" before you look at any number

Write down three categories and sort every company into one of them each quarter:

  • Acceleration: the capex growth rate increases.
  • Deceleration: capex still rises, but growth slows.
  • Contraction: absolute capex declines.

Most of the argument online happens in the middle category, which I call the bigger-but-slower zone. UBS, as reported by Reuters, projected hyperscaler capex growth of about 76% in 2026, 25% in 2027, and 6% in 2028. Under that forecast, spending rises every single year, yet by 2028 growth has collapsed by roughly 70 percentage points. If UBS is right, that is a slowdown with no year of lower spending.

Tip: Pitfall: a forecast that shows growth falling from 76% to 25% is not a spending cut. Treating "slower growth" as "less money" is the most common misreading of capex headlines.

Step 2: Pull quarterly and annual capex from primary filings

Open the cash-flow statement in each 10-Q on SEC EDGAR and record capital expenditures for at least the last eight quarters. Then run two comparisons: sequential (this quarter against last) and year over year (this quarter against the same quarter a year earlier).

Sequential numbers are noisy because chip deliveries and construction milestones arrive unevenly. Microsoft's capex went from $37.5 billion in fiscal Q2 2026 to $41 billion in fiscal Q4, a rise of roughly 9% across two quarters. Management then guided fiscal Q1 2027 above $50 billion, a jump of more than 20% in one quarter. Year-over-year figures smooth that lumpiness, so trust them more when the two disagree.

Choose your definition of capex once, and keep it. Microsoft's $41 billion includes finance leases. Its cash purchases of property and equipment were $35.8 billion, per the fiscal Q4 2026 earnings materials. Mixing those two lines across quarters manufactures a $5.2 billion swing that never happened.

Step 3: Strip out lease-accounting changes

Read the transcript for any mention of finance leases, operating leases, or reclassification before you compare periods. Lease accounting can move reported capex with no change in physical investment.

Microsoft is the live example. Its calendar-2026 capex expectation now sits near $175 billion, down from an earlier estimate of about $190 billion. The drop comes from a lease-accounting reclassification, not a decision to build less. Someone who stopped at the headline would have booked a $15 billion "cut" that the company never made.

The fix is mechanical. Restate prior periods on the new basis where the company gives enough detail. Where it doesn't, flag the quarter in your spreadsheet and don't draw a trend line across it.

Step 4: Separate short-lived chips from long-lived construction

Split each quarter's capex into servers and networking gear on one side, and land, buildings, and power on the other. GPUs, CPUs, and networking equipment wear out or go obsolete in a few years. Companies buy them close to when they need compute, so they give the earlier read on demand.

Microsoft said about two-thirds of its $41 billion fiscal Q4 spend went to short-lived assets, mainly CPUs and GPUs, which works out to roughly $27.3 billion. The long-lived side is a multi-year commitment: the company added 31 data centers that quarter, 88 for the fiscal year, and about one gigawatt of capacity.

If the short-lived share falls while construction continues, near-term compute buying may be cooling even though the total looks fine. For a read on the chip side, the Nvidia earnings breakdown shows the same demand from the supplier's view.

Step 5: Adjust for hardware price inflation

Ask what the money bought, not only how much was spent. Higher component prices raise reported capex without adding the same amount of computing capacity.

Microsoft has put a number on this. On its fiscal Q3 2026 call, it said about $25 billion of its earlier calendar-2026 estimate reflected higher component pricing. Industry data points the same way. Gartner forecasts server spending growth of 36.9% in 2026, and some of that is price, not units.

Warning: Dollar growth that outruns physical growth (gigawatts added, data centers opened) means each dollar is buying less compute. Track at least one physical metric beside every dollar figure.

Step 6: Calculate capex intensity and incremental return

Compute two ratios every quarter:

  • Capex intensity: capital expenditures divided by total revenue. A rising ratio means spending is outpacing sales.
  • Incremental return: the change in revenue divided by the change in capex over the same period. This is the strongest single productivity test available from public data.

Then look at demand signals. Microsoft Cloud revenue reached $59.3 billion in fiscal Q4 2026, up 27% from a year earlier, and Azure grew 43%. Backlog is the other gauge: commercial remaining performance obligations (contracted revenue not yet recognized) climbed to $678 billion from $627 billion a quarter earlier.

None of that proves the spending earns a good return. Companies rarely disclose AI revenue, AI profit, utilization, or return on invested capital as separate lines. Skeptics on r/AIBubble make the same point: revenue is not profit, and investor-funded demand is not the same as customers paying their own way. Alibaba shows the gap plainly. The Associated Press reported that its AI-related services revenue rose 45% while quarterly profit fell 75% as infrastructure investment climbed. Our piece on AI stocks and revenue reality goes further into that divide.

Step 7: Check cash-flow pressure

Subtract capex from operating cash flow to get free cash flow, then watch the direction over four quarters. Microsoft still generated $19.6 billion of free cash flow in fiscal Q4 2026, while flagging the drag from heavier spending.

Alphabet is further along the curve. S&P Global Market Intelligence reported that its 2026 AI spending pushed free cash flow negative, even as it raised its full-year capex forecast. A company can fund that for a while, but it shows up in more places over time: higher debt issuance, smaller buybacks, and eventually a harder look from the board at next year's budget.

Pressure on cash flow comes before the cut. That makes this step the one I weight most heavily when calling a future slowdown.

Step 8: Track guidance revisions and label forecasts as estimates

Log every guidance change, and keep company guidance separate from industry projections. Guidance is management's plan. Analyst and research-firm figures are estimates, not audited spending.

  • Gartner projects worldwide data-center spending above $650 billion in 2026, up 31.7% from nearly $500 billion in 2025.
  • Dell'Oro Group sees data-center capex reaching $1.7 trillion by 2030, with accelerated AI servers taking about two-thirds of infrastructure spend.
  • IDC ties roughly $1 trillion of infrastructure spending by 2029 to the AI build-out.

Troubleshooting

Why doesn't my capex figure match the headline?

You are probably reading cash purchases of property and equipment, while the headline includes finance leases. Microsoft's $35.8 billion versus $41 billion is exactly this gap.

Guidance went down, but management says spending is rising. Which is right?

Check for a lease reclassification. Microsoft's move from about $190 billion to about $175 billion is an accounting change, not a smaller building program.

Where do I find AI-only capex?

You won't find it. Hyperscaler totals include AI infrastructure alongside everything else. Use the short-lived asset share, cloud growth, and backlog as proxies.

The sequential numbers swing wildly. Is the trend broken?

Probably not. Deliveries arrive in lumps. Use year-over-year growth or a trailing four-quarter sum before you conclude anything.

Next steps

Run all eight steps each earnings season and keep one classification per company per quarter. On current evidence, all four big spenders are expanding, and three raised their plans in mid-2026. My call is that the bigger-but-slower zone is where this heads next. A true contraction would need falling absolute capex, confirmed by negative free cash flow spreading beyond Alphabet and by a drop in the short-lived asset share. None of that has shown up yet. Frequently asked questions

Is AI capital spending slowing right now?

Not in absolute terms. Based on mid-2026 disclosures, Alphabet and Meta raised their capex guidance, and Amazon added $20 billion in tech spending. Microsoft's lower calendar-2026 figure reflects lease accounting. Growth rates are likely to decelerate from here, with UBS projecting hyperscaler capex growth falling from about 76% in 2026 to 25% in 2027. Rising dollars with slowing growth is deceleration, not contraction.

What is the difference between a capex slowdown and a capex cut?

A slowdown means spending still rises but at a lower growth rate. A cut, or contraction, means absolute spending falls. A company that goes from 50% growth to 20% growth is spending more money than before, yet it is slowing. Mixing up these two ideas is the most common error in capex coverage.

How much revenue does AI capex need to generate to pay off?

No public figure answers this cleanly, because companies don't disclose AI-specific revenue, profit, or return on invested capital. The practical test is incremental revenue divided by incremental capex, checked alongside free cash flow. Discussions on r/wallstreetbets often build rough payback models this way. Treat any such estimate as analysis, not reported fact.

Why does Microsoft report two different capex numbers?

Microsoft reports capex including finance leases ($41 billion in fiscal Q4 2026) and cash purchases of property and equipment ($35.8 billion). The first is broader. Neither is wrong, but you must use the same one across every quarter you compare, or you will create trends that don't exist.

Which signal gives the earliest warning of a slowdown?

Free cash flow pressure and the share of spending going to short-lived assets like GPUs are the earliest signals. Companies buy chips close to when they need compute, so a falling chip share hints at cooling demand. Shrinking buybacks and rising debt issuance often show up before any formal guidance cut.
